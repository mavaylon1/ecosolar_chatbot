## How does solar work?
PV cells are built into panels. Solar panels convert sunlight into DC electricity. An inverter converts DC into AC for powering electric units in the home. The utility meter records the net amount of energy generated through the PV system. When your solar system generates more electricity than you are using, your meter spins backward and the excess electricity is sent to the electric grid, helping offset the cost of your electricity usage at night or on cloudy days.

## Will I still get a bill from the utility?
You will receive a utility bill even after installing a solar system. The bill will have the net metering information showing how much credit you have and how much energy you used from the grid (if any). The utility company usually charges a small service fee of $20–$30 per month for keeping your records and keeping you connected to the grid.

## What size solar system does an ADU need?
It depends on your goal. If you just need to pass city inspection, an ADU typically only needs a minimum-sized system — the exact minimum is informed by California's Title 24 energy code and can vary by property, so a Solar Consultant will confirm the right number for your specific inspection. A full system, sized to your actual expected usage, is usually larger. Whether the ADU will be used mainly during the day, whether you plan to add a battery, and whether you'll be charging an EV there all affect what "full size" means for you.

## Does an ADU qualify for the same incentive programs as a main house?

Combining an ADU with a sufficiently sized main-house system can sometimes make a program available; ask your Solar Consultant about your specific property.

## What are my options for paying for a solar system?
You can pay in cash, finance it, or lease it, and each works differently. With cash or financing, you own the system and typically claim the federal tax credit yourself, and you're responsible for maintenance or replacement once a component's warranty period ends. With a lease, the leasing company owns the equipment and handles everything, including repairs and replacement, but you don't personally claim the tax credit, and lease payments typically increase gradually each year (roughly 3%). At the end of a lease term, you can usually renew, buy the system outright, or have it removed. Your Solar Consultant can help you figure out which path fits your situation.

## Is the battery covered for as long as the panels?
No — batteries typically carry a shorter warranty than panels. Once battery coverage ends, repair or replacement is generally the homeowner's responsibility if you own the system outright (this doesn't apply if you're under a lease, since the leasing company remains responsible). Ask your Solar Consultant about current battery warranty terms for your system.

## When's the best time to charge an EV with solar?
Charging during daylight hours — especially mid-morning through mid-afternoon, when your system is actively producing — lets you use your own generated power directly instead of sending it back to the grid. Solar production drops off well before evening, so charging late in the day draws more from the grid than from your panels.

## Is it better to oversize my system so I produce extra power?
Not usually, unless you have a battery or an EV to use the surplus. Excess electricity your system doesn't use gets sent back to the grid, but utilities typically buy it back at a low rate (around 6-8 cents per kWh) — while pulling power back from the grid at peak times can cost you 35-45 cents per kWh. That gap is exactly why a system sized well beyond your actual usage often isn't worth the added cost unless you have a battery to store and use that extra production yourself.

## Can I add more panels to my system later?
It's best to size your system correctly from the start. Expanding an existing system later generally requires new permitting and can involve additional costs and delays, so if you expect your usage to grow (for example, adding an EV), it's worth discussing that with your Solar Consultant up front.

## Does my homeowner's insurance cover my solar system?
Many homeowner's insurance policies already include coverage for solar equipment under general property protections, such as storm or weather damage. It's worth confirming the specifics with your insurance provider.
